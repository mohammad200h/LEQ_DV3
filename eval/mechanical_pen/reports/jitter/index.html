LEAP + Xela · mechanical pen · DreamerV3 world model + DITTO

Mechanical Pen Jitter Study

Every experiment run so far on why the world model's imagined hand and the DITTO policy's commands are jerky on the mechanical-pen demos: control rate, data processing, posterior sharpness, a smoothness penalty, and a fully cleaned synthetic dataset.

Runs: 29 Oct – 12 Nov 2026Training: 50k steps per stage unless notedHardware: 2 × RTX 4090

Main result

On real observations, the policy moves its command by about 0.008 normalised units (about 0.016 rad) at every decision. That number does not change with the control rate (10–100 Hz), with how the data was processed, with how sharp the world model's posterior is, or with perfectly clean synthetic data. Only a smoothness penalty in the DITTO reward lowered it, and that cost accuracy. The decoded world-model hand behaves the same way: it adds about 0.3 mm of motion per step at every rate. Because the noise is a fixed amount per decision, jitter per second grows in direct proportion to the rate.

Ruled outData quality and preprocessing

Resampled vs. Sparsh-at-rate data and clean synthetic data all give the same per-step jitter.

Ruled outPosterior sharpness alone

A posterior with ~30% fewer likely classes halved command error but left jitter unchanged.

Still openSampling of z vs. the actor

Most-likely z cuts decoded hand jitter by 60%. The same test on the policy hasn't been run.

Background

Setup and how jitter is measured

Demonstrations are 564 labelled “Play on hand” recordings of the thumb clicking a mechanical pen. They are processed with Sparsh-skin (Xela forces, FK taxel positions, 16 joint commands as actions) and converted to DreamerV3 chunks. A world model (--algo model) is trained on forces and positions, then DITTO is trained on the frozen world model with the successful demos as the expert. 10% of episodes are held out.

Each trained policy is evaluated with rollout_leq_wm.py on 3 held-out successes, starting 0.8 s into the episode. The rollout takes the mode of the policy and of the decoder. It produces three streams:

  • Posterior: the world model filters the real episode, and the policy is queried at each real state. This is closest to deployment on the hand.
  • Prior, expert actions: the world model imagines the rest of the episode under the expert's commands. This isolates the world model's own error.
  • Prior, policy: the policy acts inside the world model's imagination.

Metrics used throughout:

  • Command change per step: mean |at − at−1| over the 16 joints, in normalised action units ([−1.5, 2.5] rad mapped to [−1, 1], so 1 unit = 2 rad).
  • Jitter ratio (× expert): the policy's command change per second divided by the expert's. This is the same as the per-step ratio.
  • Hand motion (× real): decoded fingertip-taxel movement per step divided by the real movement per step.
  • Command error: RMS difference between the policy's and the expert's commands, in rad.

Experiment 3 · 10–11 Nov · 10 Hz · 9 settings at seed 0, 4 settings at 3 seeds

Posterior-certainty sweep

On held-out data, the posterior keeps about 5 equally likely classes out of 16 per latent variable, barely fewer than the prior. So every draw of z is close to random. This sweep tried to sharpen it in three ways, alone and combined: lower rep (0.1 → 0.01), lower free bits (1.0 → 0.1 or 0), and a 3× forces decoder loss, plain, with weight decay 0.1, or with input noise.

Experiment 4 · 29 Oct · 10 Hz · seed 0 · 6k DITTO steps

Smoothness penalty in the DITTO reward

DITTO's reward was given a penalty c · |at − at−1|² with c = 10 (--ditto_smooth). Both runs used the rate-sweep 10 Hz world model and a short 6k-step DITTO schedule, so their absolute values are not comparable with the 50k-step runs.

  • It cost accuracy: command error rose by 24% on real observations. The penalty was removed afterwards, because a low-pass command filter gave similar smoothness without that cost.

Experiment 6 · 11–12 Nov · 30 Hz · 3 seeds

Clean synthetic data at 30 Hz

Analysing the raw recordings found two flaws in every real dataset. First, joint state is recorded at only about 10 Hz (commands at 30 Hz, Xela at 100 Hz), so the processing curve-fits the joints. Second, commands go out with jittery timing (11–56 ms gaps), so holding the last command on a fixed 30 Hz grid repeats 13–17% of steps and skips 12–15%. That makes the actions 2–3× jerkier than the commands as sent.

To remove both, synthetic_data.py rebuilds every episode on an exact 30 Hz grid:

  • Actions: the recorded commands interpolated over time, then smoothed with a 50 ms Gaussian. They differ from the raw commands by about 0.0005 rad on average.
  • Joints and taxel positions: those actions replayed in a headless MuJoCo with the same model and actuators as mujoco_sim_node. The hand settles for 1 s at the first action, the target ramps between steps, and collisions are off because the scene has no pen.
  • Forces: the real Xela sample nearest each step.

On the data itself, action jerk fell 6× and the worst single-step change fell from 0.53 to 0.06 rad. The world model and DITTO were trained with the standard settings.

Next steps

Recommended next tests

  1. Most-likely z on the posterior for an existing DITTO checkpoint (no retraining). If the commands become smooth, the jitter comes from z: give the actor z's probabilities or h alone. If they stay jerky, it's the actor.
  2. A behaviour-cloning term in DITTO that pulls the actor toward the expert's smooth actions on real states. Unlike the smoothness penalty, this rewards smoothness and accuracy together.
  3. Give the actor its previous command, or have it output a change in the command, so holding still becomes the easy default.
  4. Combine rep0.01 with the winner of tests 1–3. It is the best accuracy lever found so far.
  5. On the hardware, low-pass or rate-limit the commands regardless. At 10 Hz the policy jitters 5.5× the expert's rate; each doubling of the rate doubles that.
